Why Some Student Teams Work and Others Fall Apart

Almost every secondary school student has been placed in a group project at some point. For some, the experience is the best part of the term. For others, it is a source of stress, unfair grades and quiet resentment. Researchers who study classrooms say the difference between the two outcomes is rarely luck.

A 2019 review of more than 40 classroom studies found that groups did better than students working alone on complex, open-ended problems, such as designing an experiment or planning a community survey. On simple tasks with one right answer, however, the advantage mostly disappeared. In some cases, individuals were faster and just as accurate.

The researchers pointed to one factor above all others: whether each member had a clear and necessary role. When one student could finish the whole task alone, the others tended to drift. Psychologists call this "social loafing." It is not simply laziness. People put in less effort when they believe their contribution cannot be seen or does not matter.

Teachers have tried several ways to fix the problem. One common approach is to split a project into parts that depend on each other, so that no single student holds all the information. In a well-known method called the "jigsaw classroom," each student becomes an expert on one piece of a topic and must teach it to the rest of the group.

Another approach is to grade both the group product and each person's own contribution. Critics say this can turn teammates into rivals who guard their work instead of sharing it. Supporters reply that students feel the system is fairer, and fairness is what keeps them engaged.

Group work also has benefits that are harder to measure. Employers regularly list communication and teamwork among the skills they value most, and many say new graduates lack them. Explaining an idea to a classmate, disagreeing politely and reaching a shared decision are skills that are practiced, not memorized.

Still, not every student gains equally. Quieter students may be talked over, and confident students may take control. Some studies suggest that groups of three or four work better than larger ones, because every voice is more likely to be heard.

Online tools have changed the picture as well. Shared documents let teachers see who wrote what and when. That visibility can reduce free riding, but some students say it makes them feel watched rather than trusted.

In the end, the question may not be whether group work is better than working alone. The more useful question is what kind of task a group is being asked to do, and whether the task truly needs everyone at the table.
