Mei had missed the last bus twice that winter, and both times she had walked the four miles home without telling her mother.

On the third night, the driver saw her running and waited. She climbed on, out of breath, and dug through her coat for coins that were not there.

"Sit down," the driver said, without looking at her. "You can pay me on Friday."

She sat near the back, by the heater, and watched her own reflection slide over the dark shop windows. Her hands would not stop shaking.

She had been at the library until closing, rewriting an essay she had already rewritten twice, because the teacher had written one word at the top of the page: careless.

She had read that word so many times that it no longer looked like English. She wondered whether the teacher had meant the essay, or her.

The bus stopped at the hospital, and an old man got on with a paper bag of oranges. He sat across from her and offered her one. She shook her head, and then, a moment later, took it.

On Friday she brought the fare, folded flat, and held it out as she stepped on. The driver shook his head.

"Keep it," he said. "My daughter used to stay late too. She is a doctor now."

Mei did not answer. She put the coins back in her pocket. For the first time that winter she did not mind the long ride, and when she got home she told her mother about the bus, and the oranges, and the word at the top of the page.
