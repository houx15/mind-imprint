For most of the twentieth century, city planners treated parking as a basic need, like water pipes or street lights. Every new shop, office and apartment block was required to provide a fixed number of spaces, whether or not anyone would use them.

The result is visible from the air. In many American downtowns, surface parking covers more land than all the parks combined. In some mid-sized cities, researchers have counted more than five parking spaces for every resident.

Economists began to question these rules in the 1990s. Donald Shoup, a professor at the University of California, Los Angeles, argued that free parking is never really free. Its cost is hidden in the price of groceries, rent and housing, and it is paid by people who do not drive as well as by those who do.

Shoup's argument rested on a simple observation. When something valuable is given away, people use more of it than they need. Drivers circle the block looking for a free space instead of paying for a garage, and the circling itself adds traffic and pollution.

For years his ideas were treated as academic curiosities. That changed when cities started running out of money and land at the same time. A parking lot produces almost no tax revenue, while an apartment building on the same land can house hundreds of people and pay for schools and buses.

In 2017, Buffalo, New York, became the first large American city to remove parking requirements across its entire area. Developers were still allowed to build parking, but they were no longer forced to. Within a few years, several projects that would have been impossible under the old rules were completed.

Other cities followed. Minneapolis, San Jose and Austin dropped their minimum requirements, and in 2022 California passed a law that removed them near most public transit stops. Supporters said the change would lower the cost of building homes.

The early evidence suggests they were partly right. A study of Buffalo found that new developments built about half as many spaces as the old rules would have required. The land that was saved went into more housing and, in a few cases, into small public squares.

Not everyone was pleased. Some residents worried that new neighbours without garages would take the street spaces in front of their homes. Shop owners feared that customers who could not park nearby would simply drive to a suburban mall instead.

These fears are not unreasonable. In neighbourhoods with poor bus service, many families still depend on a car for work, school and medical visits. For them, a missing parking space can mean a longer, more dangerous walk at night.

Researchers who study the issue say the answer depends on what replaces the parking. Where cities have added frequent buses, safe bike lanes and shops within walking distance, car use has fallen. Where nothing else has changed, drivers have mostly moved their cars onto the street.

Some cities have gone further and turned existing lots into green space. In Seoul, an elevated highway and the parking around it were removed to restore a buried stream. The Cheonggyecheon project, completed in 2005, now draws tens of thousands of visitors a day.

Scientists who measured the area found that summer temperatures along the stream were several degrees lower than on nearby streets. Bird, fish and insect species that had disappeared from central Seoul began to return within a few years.

Paris has taken a similar path on a smaller scale. The city removed tens of thousands of on-street parking spaces and replaced many of them with trees, benches and bicycle racks. Officials argue that the changes have made the streets cooler and quieter.

Critics in Paris point out that the benefits have not been shared equally. Residents of the central districts enjoy the new green streets, while commuters from outer suburbs, who often have fewer transit options, face longer and more expensive journeys.

This tension between local benefits and wider costs appears in almost every city that has tried the idea. A park is a gift to the people who live next to it, but a parking lot served people who came from much farther away.

Planners increasingly argue that the question is not whether cars should be banned, but how much valuable land a city should give them for free. Asked that way, many residents who opposed the changes at first have come to support them.

What happens next will depend on money as much as on ideas. Turning a lot into a park costs far more than painting lines on asphalt, and cities must also pay for the buses and bike lanes that make the change work. The parking lot, it turns out, was never the cheap option it appeared to be.
